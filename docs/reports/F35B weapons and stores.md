# F-35B fields a shorter weapons list

As of 22 October 2026, the F-35B's public weapon set is the Block 3F list that services have actually named, and it is shorter than the family's station map. A U.S. Marine Corps F-35B can be shown to employ the AIM-120 AMRAAM and the two bombs crews expended for the 31 July 2015 initial operational capability, the 500 lb GBU-12 and the 1,000 lb GBU-32, with the AIM-9X carried externally in the Block 3F test record and a centerline GAU-22/A pod whose published capacity is 220 rounds. The United Kingdom, in a ministerial answer of 12 July 2022, adds ASRAAM and Paveway IV. The jet has the same eleven stations as the F-35A and F-35C, four of them internal, and a bay the program office described in 2015 as a **1,000 lb bay** against a **2,000 lb bay** on the A and C, under a **15,000 lb** weapons payload. Meteor, SPEAR 3, StormBreaker, AARGM-ER, LRASM, and the Joint Strike Missile sit in flight test or on a plan. The Director of Operational Test and Evaluation, covering events through 30 September 2025, reported that no new F-35 combat capability was fielded in fiscal 2025 and that no combat-capable TR-3 aircraft had been delivered. Door-open time, missile envelopes, and a certified external-tank size are not in the public record, so a flight-simulator specification that fills them in is adding data the sources do not contain.

## The B bay and the cleared list are both the short ones

Station count is the same on all three variants. What changes on the F-35B is internal weapon class, several station ratings, the gun installation, and the payload card. On 17 March 2015 the F-35 deputy program executive briefed the family as having **four internal and seven external weapon stations**, internal bays that combine air-to-ground and air-to-air carriage, **2,000 lb on the F-35A and F-35C and 1,000 lb on the F-35B**, and internal adapters ([RADM Mahr, Precision Strike Association, 17 March 2015](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2015/PSAR/RADMMahr.pdf)). Lockheed's March 2010 public-release brief, distribution statement A, uses the same bay split and describes six external hardpoints forward of the main landing gear plus a centerline station on every version ([Doug Hayward, Lockheed Martin, March 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/TuesdayLandmarkADougHayward.pdf)). Both that brief and the 21 March 2012 program-office roadmap print an 11-station table numbered 1 through 11 ([Col Mark Werth, 21 March 2012](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2012/annual_psr/WERTH.pdf)). An Air & Space Forces Magazine profile's "10 stations," and a 2019 article's narration of "12 stations," disagree with the program-office count of four plus seven. The magazine figure is a secondary profile of the F-35A and includes a 22,000 lb carriage claim that the product cards do not give the B ([Air & Space Forces Magazine, F-35 Lightning II](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/weapons/f-35/)).

The text layer of the 2010 and 2012 slides scrambles left-to-right column order, so the table below is a reading of the extracted pound figures anchored by later prose, not a sentence that says "station 2 is the outboard pylon." Lockheed has named **stations 4 and 8** as the internal bay stations that a Sidekick adapter would use for two AIM-120-class missiles each ([The War Zone, quoting Lockheed Martin](https://www.twz.com/adapter-for-f-35-internal-carriage-of-six-aim-120-missiles-is-progressing)). Greg Ulmer, then Lockheed's F-35 general manager, named **stations 3 and 9**, "the two closest to the fuselage," as the wing stations already piped to accept fuel ([Air & Space Forces Magazine, 17 June 2019](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/lockheed-looking-at-extending-the-f-35s-range-weapons-suite/)). No sentence in the sources says which number is port and which is starboard. A 2012 flight-test release puts an inert AIM-9X on the F-35B's starboard pylon and does not give a station number ([NAVAIR, 21 June 2012](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/17256)). The published limits are symmetric, so side does not change a B load rating.

The 2010 and 2012 slides still say, in prose, that the STOVL bay weapon class is 1,000 lb and the CTOL/CV class is 2,000 lb ([Hayward, March 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/TuesdayLandmarkADougHayward.pdf); [Werth, 21 March 2012](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2012/annual_psr/WERTH.pdf)). That class limit and the station rating are different numbers. A GBU-32 at **1,013 lb** launch weight for the (v)1/B sits in the program's 1,000 lb class and is 13 lb over a literal reading of "1,000" ([U.S. Air Force JDAM fact sheet](https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104572/joint-direct-attack-munition-gbu-313238/)). A GBU-31 at **2,036 lb** or **2,115 lb**, depending on variant, fits inside a 2,500 lb rating on paper and still contradicts the 1,000 lb bay sentence. The 2010-versus-2012 disagreement on whether stations 4 and 8 are 1,500 lb or 2,500 lb on the STOVL jet should stay open. It does not raise the bay class. Two readings of the same 2012 roadmap also disagree, because the text layer is scrambled: one reading limits the B's Block 3 internal line to four AIM-120Cs and keeps GBU-31 and JSOW off the B chart, and another finds GBU-31, JSOW, Paveway IV, and ASRAAM named on the B chart without a reliable internal-versus-external label. The 2015 brief states the bay class in a sentence, and that sentence is the one to follow.

The same reconstruction, which is not a single published loadout card, is how the internal combinations fit. Each bay has one heavier station and one lighter station. The 2012 Block 2B STOVL line, "2 GBU-12, 2 GBU-32, 2 AMRAAM," marked as an internal set, is a list of types. Four bombs at once would need four air-to-ground stations. Lockheed's variant material gives the B a standard internal load of **two AIM-120C/D and two 1,000 lb GBU-32s**, and gives the A and C **two AIM-120C/D and two 2,000 lb GBU-31s** ([Lockheed Martin F-35 variant presentation](https://view.ceros.com/lockheed-martin/f-35-variants), undated, retrieved 22 October 2026). The air-to-air arrangement in the 2012 B Block 3 line, and in Lockheed's 2019 description of the pre-Sidekick bay, is **four AIM-120s, two per bay**, using the heavier stations when they are not holding bombs. A Marine Corps or program-office sentence that states "four" as a cleared B load, with a date after IOC, was not found. The counted IOC expenditure is the bombs. Sidekick, which would put a third AIM-120 in each A and C bay for six internally, was described in May 2019 as incompatible with the B because the bay is smaller ([Seapower, 1 May 2019](https://seapowermagazine.org/lockheed-develops-rack-to-make-f-35a-c-a-six-shooter/); [UPI, 3 May 2019](https://www.upi.com/Defense-News/2019/05/03/Lockheeds-Sidekick-adds-increased-firepower-to-F-35-fighters/6201556888721/)). A later Lockheed statement said the adapter was on contract. It was not described as operational, on the A and C or on the B. The 2023 product cards say Block 4 includes "increased missile-carriage capacity" and give no count and no statement that the B bay grew ([F-35B product card, 2023](https://www.f35.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/aero/f35/documents/F-35B%20Product%20Card.pdf)). No post-2015 primary sentence was found that repeats "1,000 lb on F-35B," and nothing found says the bay was later enlarged.

The B weapons payload on the Lockheed card is **15,000 lb (6,800 kg)** on the 2015, 2019, and 2023 cards. Internal fuel on the 2023 card is **13,100 lb (5,942 kg)**. The A and C weapons payload is **18,000 lb (8,160 kg)** ([F-35A product card](https://www.f35.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/aero/f35/documents/F-35A%20Product%20Card.pdf); [F-35C product card](https://www.f35.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/aero/f35/documents/F-35C%20Product%20Card.pdf); [F-35B card, 2015](https://www.f35.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/aero/f35/documents/fg15-1280_002_f-35b_pc.pdf)). Those cards are easy to mix with fuel. A 2010 comparison slide states CTOL internal fuel as 18,483 lb, while the 2023 A card says 18,250 lb, and a 2010 stealth-features slide places 18,500 lb, 14,000 lb, and 20,000 lb beside "Large Capacity Internal Fuel Tanks" for CTOL, STOVL, and CV. Those round numbers are fuel callouts. Using 14,000 lb as the B weapons payload would contradict both the 15,000 lb card and the 13,100 lb fuel figure. The station-slide headline "Over 18K lbs Of Payload Capability" is a family line on a page that also shows the B's lower ratings. An editorial infographic prints **3,500 lb** internal and **15,000 lb** internal-plus-external for the B, beside 5,400 lb internal and 18,000 lb combined for the A and C ([Breaking Defense infographic](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/2097098/F-35_infographic_R8.pdf)). The 15,000 lb figure matches the card. The 3,500 lb figure is not on the card. Another pass through that file's text extract could not tie every weapon row to a variant reliably, so the graphic is support for counts that JPO and Lockheed also describe, and it is not authority for rows that lack a second source.

Adding the STOVL column as read from 2010 (300, 1,500, 5,000, 1,500, 350, and 1,000 lb, each twice except the centerline) produces **18,300 lb**. Reading stations 4 and 8 as 2,500 lb instead produces **20,300 lb**. Both sums are larger than the 15,000 lb card. The card and the station table were not tied together by a worked loadout in the documents reviewed, so a specification has to enforce the station ratings and the 15,000 lb card as separate limits. "Beast mode" totals of **5,700 lb internal** and **22,000 lb combined** come from a 2017 Lockheed graphic about RAAF F-35As ([f35.com](https://www.f35.com/f35/news-and-features/raaf-flies-f35as-in-beastmode-for-the-first-time.html)). A 22 July 2020 training load at Luke, six inert GBU-12s and one AIM-9X training missile on an F-35A, is a heavy bring-back for students, and the unit did not call it the structural maximum ([Air Education and Training Command](https://www.aetc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/2303929/63rd-fs-implements-beast-mode-weapons-configuration-on-f-35a/)). No B beast-mode photograph caption or station list was found.

External fuel is a plumbing fact and a missing store. Ulmer said in June 2019 that an early requirement for 480-gallon tanks on the inboard wing stations had been dropped, that stations 3 and 9 were already piped, and that 600-gallon tanks were then a study with no timeline. The 2010 carriage graphic's extracted text includes a 426-gallon wing tank among stores to be certified during system development, and that extraction is only as reliable as a scrambled graphic. It disagrees with 480. As of 30 June 2025, The War Zone reported that external tanks had never been fielded, that 2004–2007 studies of 480- and 460-gallon underwing tanks were abandoned, and that the Air Force FY2026 budget asked for a feasibility evaluation. A Defense Department statement reported the next day said the department would explore feasibility of external tanks, including under-wing tanks, for all three variants ([The War Zone, 30 June 2025, updated 1 July 2025](https://www.twz.com/air/plans-to-finally-give-f-35-external-fuel-tanks-emerge-in-new-air-force-bud)). A program-office sentence given to that outlet, that there is no approved requirement for auxiliary internal fuel tanks, is about internal tanks. No official empty mass, length, diameter, or certified gallonage was found. A tank, if one were ever cleared, would occupy station 3 or 9 and displace the weapon on that station. No source was found that any other station is plumbed, or that the centerline can carry a tank, a bomb, or a targeting pod.

One store occupies one station. Stations 1 and 11 are the 300 lb air-to-air pair. Stations 5 and 7 are the 350 lb air-to-air pair. Station 6 is the B gun pod or empty. Stations 2 and 10 are the B's 1,500 lb outboard pair. A bomb on station 4 is the ordinary reason that station cannot also hold the AIM-120 of a four-missile load. An AIM-120C/D at 356 or 358 lb is a few pounds over the printed 350 lb cell, in the same way the loaded pod at 1,017 lb is over the printed 1,000 lb cell. The four-missile arrangement is still the published air-to-air reading. A specification should keep both published numbers. The gun pod and an internal load are different stations. The pod was designed with a weapon-bay-door bumper, so it sits next to the doors ([Douglas Parker, General Dynamics, 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/TuesdayLandmarkADouglasParker.pdf)). No published rule says the pod deletes the bay, and no published clearance says the pod and a full internal load have been released together. Absence of a prohibition is not a clearance. The same is true of a pod plus tanks on stations 3 and 9: different stations, no published mutual exclusion, and no tank cleared to hang there. Air-to-ground stations were described in 2010 and 2015 as MIL-STD-1760C Class 1, air-to-air stations as tailored Class 1, and the centerline as structural and tailored Class 1 on STOVL, with non-pyrotechnic suspension and release. Those are interface statements. Stay-out volumes inside the bay are described for the weapon, the fall path, maintainer access, and the flow field, and no inch, millimeter, or degree value for those volumes was printed.

### What public sources will support on an operational F-35B

"Cleared" here means a service or ministry has said the jet is cleared to employ the store, or operational crews expended it for initial operational capability. It does not mean a score from the classified combined Block 3F initial operational test report that DOT&E published in February 2024. Unclassified annual reports omit those effectiveness results. The latest unclassified cut, the FY2025 F-35 chapter submitted to Congress on 13 March 2026 and covering events through 30 September 2025, says **no new combat capability was fielded in FY2025** and that **no combat-capable TR-3 aircraft had been delivered**. By the end of September 2025, **158** TR-3 jets had been accepted with truncated software that disabled some combat capabilities already fielded on TR-2. Software builds 40R01 and 40R02 were designated not suitable for operational test ([DOT&E FY2025 Annual Report](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2025/Other/2025Annual-Report.pdf); [DOT&E, 16 March 2026](https://www.dote.osd.mil/News/News-Display/Article/4435570/fy-2025-dote-annual-report-submitted-to-congress/)).

The Marine Corps declared VMFA-121 operational on **31 July 2015** with ten Block 2B F-35Bs and referred to weapons clearances without naming stores ([U.S. Marine Corps, 31 July 2015](https://www.marines.mil/News/News-Display/Article/611657/us-marines-corps-declares-the-f-35b-operational)). The wing's expenditure article says 14 pilots in six F-35Bs employed **18 GBU-12s and 12 GBU-32s**, inert and high-explosive, on 22–26 June, and that this was one of the last IOC prerequisites. The extracted text does not print the year ([3rd Marine Aircraft Wing](https://www.3rdmaw.marines.mil/Media-Room/Stories/News-Article-Display/Article/606566/marines-on-target-with-f-35-live-weapons-release/)). In May 2014 a Lockheed business-development director told USNI that the Block 2B configuration intended for that declaration included AIM-120, the 500 lb GBU-12, and the 1,000 lb GBU-32 ([USNI News, 2 June 2014](https://news.usni.org/2014/06/02/marine-joint-strike-fighter-track-meet-2015-goal)). AIM-9X, the gun, SDB, and JSOW are outside that IOC expenditure. On **12 July 2022** the UK Ministry of Defence answered that the UK F-35B was cleared to employ **Paveway IV, AMRAAM, and ASRAAM**, and that future uplifts would add SPEAR Capability 3 and Meteor ([UK Parliament, UIN 31755](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2022-07-06/31755)). The page itself returned HTTP 403 on fetch, so that wording is the search extract of the official page. The undated RAF Lightning page lists Meteor with AMRAAM, ASRAAM, and Paveway IV, and gives land IOC as December 2018 and maritime IOC as December 2020 ([Royal Air Force, Lightning F-35B](https://www.raf.mod.uk/aircraft/current-aircraft/lightning-f35b/)). The Meteor line conflicts with the 2022 answer and with later ministerial timelines. The dated government statements govern. The Italian Navy declared initial operational capability for its embarked F-35B component on 26 August 2024, announced 6 September 2024, and named counter-air, strike, and suppression of enemy air defenses without naming a weapon ([Marina Militare, 6 September 2024](https://www.marina.difesa.it/EN/Conosciamoci/notizie/Pagine/20240906_Italian_Carrier_Strike_Group_Achievement_of_Initial_Operational.aspx)). A secondary account says an Italian F-35B employed GBU-12 and fired AIM-120C. That account is not an official list ([Alert 5, 22 September 2024](https://alert5.com/2024/09/22/italian-navys-transition-to-the-f-35b-a-unique-approach/)). Lockheed's 2023 card names the U.S. Marine Corps, the Royal Air Force, and the Italian Air Force as F-35B operators.

The Block 3F weapons plan, preserved in a DOT&E assessment copied by the Australian Parliament while flight test was still expected to finish in 2017, was to employ the GBU-12, GBU-31/32, JSOW-C1, SDB I, AIM-120C, AIM-9X, and the gun "in the full operating environment of each variant" ([Australian Parliament document store](https://www.aph.gov.au/DocumentStore.ashx?id=7dc1ba4b-4efb-4354-b25f-d26a1d25dfd5)). The phrase "each variant" does not override the 1,000 lb B bay. An Air Force article on the end of that campaign, with no dateline in the extracted text, says testing began in July 2013, used all three variants, and delivered AIM-120, AIM-9X, UK ASRAAM, Paveway IV, GBU-39, GBU-12, GBU-31, and AGM-154, then GAU-22 events, **55 weapons excluding the gun**. It says squadrons receiving 3F software could load those weapons, and it does not split results by variant ([U.S. Air Force](https://www.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/1400908/f-35-testers-wrap-up-weapons-delivery-accuracy-tests/)). A campaign that included a store is a different fact from a 2026 clearance on the B.

Block 4 is the schedule behind every row marked as a plan. GAO reported on 3 September 2025 that Block 4 began as a 66-capability effort the program said in 2018 it would deliver by 2026, that a smaller rebaselined subprogram was expected **no earlier than 2031**, and that many original capabilities would not reach the fleet until the mid-2030s. Weapons were among the categories officials said would remain inside the reduced subprogram. As of May 2025 the program office could not give GAO the approved list of what survived the cut. Program officials told GAO that Lockheed planned to begin delivering combat-capable TR-3 aircraft in 2026 ([GAO-25-107632](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107632/index.html)). Nothing in the sources confirms or denies those deliveries between 1 October 2025 and 22 October 2026. A UK Defence Journal account in April 2026 still used a completion date around 2029 and a cost near $16.5 billion, which matches the pre-rebaseline figures ([UK Defence Journal, 21 April 2026](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/meteor-f-35-integration-shows-no-signs-of-urgency/)). GAO's 3 September 2025 report is the later date. Full-rate production was approved in March 2024, and that decision split Block 4 into its own major subprogram ([DOT&E FY2024 F-35](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2024/dod/2024f-35jsf.pdf)). Production approval did not add a combat load. An unclassified symposium paper by the F-35 partner-weapons lead says the enterprise was averaging more than 90 months from a formal weapons requirement to fleet release. It is a process note, not a store-by-store status list ([Naval Postgraduate School symposium paper](https://www.dair.nps.edu/bitstream/123456789/5586/1/SYM-AM-26-142.pdf)).

## One pod, 220 rounds, and two published fire rates

Every variant uses the **25 mm four-barrel GAU-22/A**. On the F-35A the gun is internal. On the F-35B and F-35C it is a centerline missionized pod, and the pods do not interchange: DOT&E states that outer-mold-line differences make each pod unique to its variant, so **an F-35B gun pod cannot be mounted on an F-35C** ([DOT&E FY2018](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2018/dod/2018f35jsf.pdf); [DOT&E FY2019](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2019/dod/2019f35jsf.pdf?ver=2020-01-30-115432-173)). A secondary article assigns the designations GPU-9/A to the B and GPU-8/A to the C, citing a contracting notice, and quotes a February 2024 DOT&E passage that a podded GAU-22/A "is available" for the B and C on the centerline ([The War Zone](https://www.twz.com/air/f-35b-and-f-35c-use-different-25mm-gun-pods-unique-to-each-variant)). The FY2018 and FY2019 reports state the pods are unique and do not use those designations. Another press account uses GPU-9/A beside a 180-round figure for the A. The designation to implement, until a government fact sheet prints one, is a B-specific missionized pod.

The installation numbers from 2010 and the gun-maker's later sheet do not match. Lockheed in March 2010 published **3,000 shots per minute**, a four-barrel derivative of the GAU-12, an internal linear linkless feed of **181 rounds** on CTOL, and a pod helical feed of **220 rounds**, then qualified with PGU-20/U API and PGU-23 training-practice ammunition. The primary mission line on that slide is air-to-ground, with air-to-air as a fallout capability. Gun qualification had been completed in 2008, and the pod was in a 36,000-round qualification program ([Hayward, March 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/TuesdayLandmarkADougHayward.pdf)). General Dynamics' 2010 missionized-gun brief repeats 3,000 shots per minute and 220 rounds, and gives the loaded pod as **1,017 lb**, **27 inches wide, 32 inches high, and 146 inches long**, with a 4,000 psi hydraulic system and structure by Terma. A 220-round fire-out was a listed test point. It is not a published limit on a combat burst. Engineering testing of the missionized system was reported complete in May 2009. A June 2010 "qualification complete" entry on that brief was an upcoming milestone, not a completion report ([Parker, 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/TuesdayLandmarkADouglasParker.pdf)).

The manufacturer's 2024 sheet states a gun capability, and it does not state an F-35 fire-control setting. It says **up to 3,300 shots per minute**, gun weight **230 lb (104.3 kg)**, dispersion **5 milliradians diameter, 80 percent circle (1.4 milliradians, 1-sigma radius)**, muzzle velocity **3,560 ft/s (1,085 m/s)** for TP and HEI ammunition and **3,400 ft/s (1,036 m/s)** for API, and average recoil **3,700 lb**. The sheet says the gun was developed for both the internal and the external installation, and it does not restate 220 rounds or the pod envelope ([GD-OTS GAU-22/A, 2024.08](https://www.gdots.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/400001948-GAU-22-A-2024-09-V04.pdf)). Air Combat Command, writing on 24 August 2018 about the F-35A after the first operational load-and-fire training at Hill, said the internal cannon carries 181 rounds and can fire at **up to 50 rounds per second**, which is 3,000 per minute ([Air Combat Command, 24 August 2018](https://www.acc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/1616527/388th-fw-dialing-in-cannon-loading-firing-in-training/)). That sentence is the A internal gun. **Which of the two rates the B pod uses in service is not published.** Two hundred twenty rounds at 3,000 per minute is 4.4 seconds if the magazine is fired in one continuous burst. That arithmetic is not a published burst limit.

The A magazine is 181 in more than one primary account: Lockheed in 2010, a 14 August 2015 full-capacity firing from AF-2 of 181 PGU-23/U rounds, and a 2020 Luke loader caption in which each magazine holds 181 rounds. The loader holds four magazines, 724 rounds. That 724 is the loader. The aircraft figure in the same caption is 181 ([USNI News, 20 August 2015](https://news.usni.org/2015/08/20/video-181-rounds-fired-from-f-35a-lighting-ii-joint-strike-fighter-gatling-gun); [Air Education and Training Command, Luke AFB](https://www.aetc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/2417494/armament-shop-maintains-f-35-weapons-system-equipment/)). A later press account of a program-office spokesperson says the A magazine is 180 rounds and names PGU-48/B as the operational round ([The War Zone](https://www.twz.com/air/f-35as-beleaguered-25mm-cannon-is-finally-effective)). **181 and 180 disagree. The full-capacity firing is 181.** The 180-round claim is second-hand, and the same is true of calling PGU-48/B the current operational issue. Rounds DOT&E itself names in the gun-test writeups are PGU-23 training practice, PGU-32/U, PGU-47/U, and PGU-48/B. Air-to-ground flight tests of the last three, from all three variants at China Lake in August through December 2017, have classified target-damage results ([DOT&E FY2018](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2018/dod/2018f35jsf.pdf)). The 2024 sheet names TP, HEI, and API classes and does not give PGU stock numbers. Empty-pod weight was not found. The 230 lb figure is the gun alone. The slide's 1,000 lb centerline cell and the 1,017 lb loaded pod both stand.

Installed accuracy is a different number from the gun sheet, and the contract's numeric accuracy limit is not printed. DOT&E's FY2017 report says F-35A air-to-ground testing showed a consistent long-and-to-the-right aiming bias ([DOT&E FY2017](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2017/dod/2017f35jsf.pdf)). Through the FY2018 and FY2019 reports, DOT&E called F-35A installed accuracy unacceptable: system-development testing had failed the contract specification, mount misalignments meant the true alignment of each A-model gun was not known, and no software or hardware correction had yet fixed it. Newer A-model jets showed coating and chine-longeron cracks near the muzzle, and the Air Force had restricted the gun on Lot 9 and newer aircraft to combat use only. **F-35B and F-35C pod results were described as consistent and as meeting the contract specification.** Pod activity through July 2018 was 13 strafe missions and 2,695 rounds of PGU-23 and PGU-32. Whether the Lot 9 restriction on the A was later lifted was not established from a primary source in this record. An installed B-pod dispersion in milliradians was not published. The manufacturer's 5 milliradian figure is the gun specification, repeated in 2010 and 2024. The A-model bias shows that aircraft alignment can dominate that specification. It is a finding about the internal gun. Muzzle velocity on the 2024 sheet is an ammunition-class figure, not a measurement from an F-35 in flight. A 2012 NAVAIR release shows F-35B BF-2 airborne with the centerline pod, a GBU-32 and an AIM-120 in the starboard bay, and an AIM-9X on the starboard pylon. That is early flight-test geometry, not a cleared maximum load ([NAVAIR, 21 June 2012](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/17256)).

## AMRAAM is the radar missile the jet is cleared to fly

Raytheon states that the newest AMRAAM is operational on all three F-35 variants and that it is **the only radar-guided air-to-air missile cleared to fly on the F-35** ([RTX AMRAAM](https://www.rtx.com/raytheon/what-we-do/air/amraam-missile)). The page does not tie the phrase "newest version" to the AIM-120D-3 suffix. A photo caption on the same page identifies an AIM-120D-3 launched from an F/A-18F. The Navy fact file, updated 23 September 2021, says AIM-120A/B/C/D are operational on Navy, Marine Corps, and Air Force aircraft and lists F-35B/C among platforms. It does not say which dash number is cleared in an F-35 bay, and it does not separate internal from external stations ([U.S. Navy AIM-120 fact file](https://www.navy.mil/Resources/Fact-Files/Display-FactFiles/Article/2168352/aim-120-advanced-medium-range-air-to-air-missile-amraam/)). NAVAIR's product page likewise lists the F-35B/C and gives one guidance label, active radar, with speed and range marked classified ([NAVAIR AMRAAM](https://www.navair.navy.mil/product/AMRAAM)). Block 3F test language says the jet is intended to employ **AIM-120C** ([FY2017 DOT&E F-35 report copy](http://www.aereo.jor.br/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/F-35-report-2017.pdf)). Lockheed's undated variant page puts AIM-120C/D in the standard internal load. DOT&E's FY2023 AMRAAM chapter says F-35A/B/C can employ AMRAAM, that AIM-120D3 finished integrated testing in May 2023 as a form-fit hardware refresh of the D family, and that a fielding recommendation was still pending service approval late in FY2023 ([DOT&E FY2023 AMRAAM](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2023/af/2023amraam.pdf)). The first F-35 firing of AIM-120D3, on developmental software 30R08.051, was from an **F-35C** on 14 November 2023, in the same event series as AIM-120D, AIM-9X, and AIM-9X-3 ([AFOTEC, 14 November 2023](https://www.afotec.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/3587984/uott-conducts-8-live-fire-missile-tests/)). A manufacturer sentence, a pending fielding recommendation, and a developmental shot from the C are three different statuses. None of them publishes a B-bay clearance for a named suffix. The UK answer says "AMRAAM" with no dash number. Public F-35 language to implement is the family name AMRAAM, with AIM-120C as the Block 3F designation and C/D as the pair on Lockheed's standard-load page. No source reviewed names AIM-120B, C-5, C-7, or C-8 as the specific round cleared in the B bay. The A/B airframes have a larger published span than C/D, and no source says the B-model missile is cleared in the bay.

The internal count is four, as the pre-Sidekick two-per-bay arrangement, with the caveats in the station section. No official sheet states a maximum of external AIM-120s on the B, or a full air-to-air total that adds underwing AMRAAMs to four internal missiles and two AIM-9Xs. The editorial infographic's external menu for the B stops at two AIM-9Xs plus bombs and the gun, and its internal B column lists four AIM-120s, or two AIM-120s with two GBU-12s, two GBU-32s, two GBU-49s, or eight StormBreakers marked as planned integration. GBU-49 on that graphic has no clearance sentence elsewhere. The StormBreaker count is a plan, treated below. An AIM-120 at 356 or 358 lb is over the 300 lb wingtip rating, so the published wingtip cell is not an AMRAAM station even before any clearance question.

AIM-9X separation testing for Block 3F was **external only for all variants**. F-35C work included a redesigned outboard wing structure after excessive loads with the AIM-9X on the outboard external pylons. A December 2016 weapons-delivery event scored "AIM-9X BLOCK 2" as successful ([FY2017 DOT&E F-35 report copy](http://www.aereo.jor.br/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/F-35-report-2017.pdf)). The Air Force campaign wrap-up includes the AIM-9X among weapons squadrons could load with 3F software, without a variant split. The 2012 B flight carried the missile on a wing pylon. The infographic prints two AIM-9X as the external air-to-air option and does not list AIM-9X internally. Nothing reviewed clears AIM-9X inside the bay. Nothing in an MoD or program-office sentence says the B carriage is wingtip-only. The C's outboard-pylon loads show that "external" has included the outboard wing, at least on that variant. AIM-9X at 186 lb fits the 300 lb wingtip by weight and also fits the 1,500 lb outboard rating. AIM-9X-3 was fired in the November 2023 developmental test. The article does not say it was fleet-released, does not name the station, and does not define how "AIM-9X-3" maps to Block II nomenclature.

ASRAAM is the UK missile in the July 2022 clearance sentence, and it was already in the Block 3F campaign. In the FY2017 DOT&E text, two UK ASRAAM weapons-delivery events, in February and June 2017, were still "analysis in progress." The later undated wrap-up lists ASRAAM among weapons delivered. The clearance sentence is the 2022 answer, which says ASRAAM and does not say Block 6. A 16 August 2016 Ministry of Defence release funded an updated stockpile so F-35s could use the missile beyond 2022, put integration on a separate contract, and expected the updated missile on RAF and Royal Navy F-35s from 2022. It calls ASRAAM a heat-seeking weapon and does not use the label Block 6 or name a station ([GOV.UK, 16 August 2016](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/mod-signs-184-million-contract-to-secure-air-to-air-missiles-for-the-f-35)). A secondary report of a Quin answer says ASRAAM Block 6 was due on Typhoon in 2022 and on F-35 in 2024. The Hansard page for that answer was not retrieved, and no 2024, 2025, or 2026 confirmation was found that Block 6, as distinct from ASRAAM, is in service on the F-35B ([UK Defence Journal](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/asraam-block-6-to-enter-service-on-f-35-and-typhoon-by-2024/)). MBDA lists the F-35 among platforms and lists the UK and Australia as users. It does not say Australia's F-35 carries ASRAAM, and no source found says Italy's or the Marine Corps' F-35B does ([MBDA ASRAAM](https://www.mbda-systems.com/products/air-dominance/asraam)). A secondary programme history says an internal-ASRAAM plan was dropped in favor of two missiles on stations 1 and 11. That compilation is the only source in this record that names those stations. No MoD sentence assigns them. The published mass is 88 kg, which converts to about 194 lb, so ASRAAM is inside the 300 lb wingtip rating by weight. Fit is not a station clearance.

Meteor on the F-35B is a bay trial, led by the UK, with Italy sponsoring integration on the F-35A. The Royal Air Force said on 28 February 2025 that a U.S. Marine Corps F-35B had flown the first test flights with an inert Meteor from NAS Patuxent River to gather environmental data, and that the missile was not described as operational ([Royal Air Force, 28 February 2025](https://www.raf.mod.uk/news/articles/meteors-first-flight-on-an-f-35b/); [MBDA, 28 February 2025](https://www.mbda-systems.com/meteors-first-flight-f-35b)). The Royal Navy, on 6 March 2025, said the trials had seen Meteor loaded in the weapons bay for the first time and "the launch of an inert missile," flown by Lt Cdr Nick Baker ([Royal Navy, 6 March 2025](https://www.royalnavy.mod.uk/news/2025/march/06/250306-meteor-missile-trialed-on-f35-jet)). The RAF release of the week before describes environmental flights and does not say the missile left the aircraft. Photo captions relayed by trade press put the first sortie on 20 November 2024, aircraft BF-18. Those details are not on the RAF news page. Press descriptions of the imagery show one Meteor in a bay beside an AIM-120, with cropped fins. The War Zone's statement that fighters will carry a maximum of four Meteors, all internally, is the magazine's claim. It is not in the RAF, Royal Navy, or MBDA releases. Maria Eagle's written answer of 9 June 2025 puts in-service capability in the **early 2030s**, driven by the U.S.-led program office ([TheyWorkForYou, 9 June 2025](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/wrans/?id=2025-05-13.52037.h)). A 20 April 2026 reply by Luke Pollard, as reported the next day, says the estimate remains the early 2030s. The written answer itself was not fetched ([UK Defence Journal, 21 April 2026](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/meteor-f-35-integration-shows-no-signs-of-urgency/)). MBDA's 2023 datasheet says Meteor is being integrated on the F-35A and F-35B, shows a full-fin and a cropped-fin variant, and does not say which fin is the F-35 store. It does state rail and ejection launch ([MBDA Meteor datasheet, 2023-08-v03](https://mbdainc.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/2023-METEOR-datasheet.pdf)). MBDA has also described F-35A ground vibration and fit checks at Edwards, with flight testing still ahead. No source gives Italian F-35Bs a Meteor clearance ahead of the UK-led B campaign. The published mass is 190 kg, which converts to about 419 lb, so Meteor is over the 350 lb internal air-to-air station and over the 300 lb wingtip. It fits the heavier internal stations by the published ratings. That weight comparison is consistent with a bay store beside an AIM-120. It is not a station assignment.

AIM-260 is a Lockheed follow-on the company says will be used on the F-22 and the F-35, with greater range than existing air-to-air missiles and no number given ([Reuters, 17 September 2026](https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/lockheed-martin-reveals-new-secret-missile-fast-tracks-production-2026-09-17/)). A magazine page lists F-35 integration as planned, initial operational capability as not achieved, and dimensions as unknown ([Air & Space Forces Magazine, AIM-260](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/weapons/aim-260-jatm/)). No government or Lockheed fact sheet in the sources gives length, diameter, span, mass, guidance class, or range.

Published guidance classes, and nothing finer, are active radar with inertial midcourse updates for AIM-120, infrared for AIM-9X with the phrase "imaging infrared" used for Block II, heat-seeking infrared for ASRAAM in the 2016 Ministry release, and active radar with inertial midcourse and a data link for Meteor. The current MBDA ASRAAM page does not print a guidance-class label. It does say lock-on before launch and lock-on after launch. Meteor's ramjet is the propulsion description on the datasheet, a solid-fuel variable-flow ducted rocket, set against AMRAAM's solid-propellant rocket on the Navy fact file. AIM-260's guidance class is not officially published.

## Standoff stores are captive, planned, or sized for the A and C

The bombs a B can be shown to release are the 500 lb and 1,000 lb classes. **GBU-32** is the published standard internal bomb, two per aircraft, one on the heavier station in each bay. **GBU-12**, the 500 lb Paveway II, is the other bomb in the Block 2B internal set and in the IOC expenditure. The Air Force art caption calls the GBU-12 a 500 lb laser-guided bomb, **10 feet 9 inches** long, with a diameter given only as **"11 to 18 in,"** which does not separate body diameter from fin span ([U.S. Air Force art caption](https://www.af.mil/News/Art/igtag/bombs/)). The JDAM family on the current Air Force fact sheet, including GBU-31, GBU-32, and GBU-38, is GPS-aided inertial. That sheet's clearance prose is stale: it still says follow-on F-35 integration is underway or planned, and its aircraft list omits the F-35. The dimensions are usable. The clearance sentence is not a 2026 status. GBU-31(v)1/B is **152.7 in and 2,036 lb**. GBU-31(v)3/B is **148.6 in and 2,115 lb**. Family wingspan is **25 in**. GBU-32(v)1/B is **119.5 in and 1,013 lb**, wingspan **19.6 in**. GBU-38 is **92.6 in and 558 lb**, wingspan **14 in**. Body diameter is not on the sheet. A secondary compilation's diameters are omitted here because that page warns that its own figures may be inaccurate. The 2012 roadmap identifies the B's 1,000 lb JDAM with an Mk 83 or BLU-110 bomb body, and the A/C 2,000 lb JDAM with an Mk 84 or BLU-109 body. Those are the published store identities. External clearance of a GBU-31 on the B, as distinct from a scrambled 2012 chart label, was not found. GBU-38 and GBU-54 appear on the 17 March 2015 brief as proposed Block 4 growth, not as cleared system-development stores, and the brief does not assign variant or bay ([Mahr, 17 March 2015](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2015/PSAR/RADMMahr.pdf)). DOT&E FY2025 records one GBU-54 employment by the operational test team after a limited test resume in October 2024. Guidance class for GBU-54 was not stated in the pages retrieved. GBU-49 appears on the editorial infographic and not in a clearance statement.

**Paveway IV** is the UK bomb. On 12 June 2015, RAF test pilot Andy Edgell released two inert 500 lb Paveway IVs from F-35B BF-03. The Pax River test force called the separation an internal-bay event and called Paveway IV "the first dual mode bomb operational on the F-35," which is the test team's wording at first separation ([F-35 Pax River ITF, DVIDS](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/167123/f-35b-lightning-ii-aces-first-release-uk-paveway-iv-bomb)). The ministerial clearance sentence is 12 July 2022. UK MOD Form 706D(PWIV)(F-35), established April 2025, is the preparation and air-carriage record. Its weapon block has live and practice, standard and "Tac Pen," and **internal and external**. The form also has columns for STOVL takeoffs, STOVL landings, air-carriage hours, and **inboard and outboard door cycles**. The instructions say the numeric lives come from the Joint Services Munitions Constraint Register ([MOD Form 706D](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/680b6a73faff81833fcae8c6/706D_PWIV__F-35_.pdf); [MOD Form 799](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/680b6d1956bc2cfe7f7f5b8e/799_PWIV__F-35_.pdf)). For this weapon, internal carriage, external carriage, and STOVL flight with the store aboard are expected, life-counted conditions. The register values were not on the form. A 500 lb test description and a secondary "225 kg" description are the same class. They are not a traced all-up weight with the guidance kit. Primary length, body diameter, and fin span were not retrieved. An unsourced secondary page's 3.1 m length is not used. The 2015 release calls the bomb dual-mode and names inertial navigation and GPS. It does not use the word laser in the text retrieved. A secondary account identifies semi-active laser as the second mode ([Think Defence](https://www.thinkdefence.co.uk/docs/paveway-iv/)). Whether that second mode is in the F-35 operational flight program, as distinct from the weapon's general design, was not said in the test release.

The door facts that are public are an angle and a sequence, not a time. Code One, a Lockheed publication, says the inboard weapon-bay doors open **ninety-seven degrees** to launch AMRAAMs and open to **about forty-five degrees** in a vertical landing, forming side walls for the lift-fan fountain, with a stated ten-percent vertical-lift benefit. It also says the A and C bays are slightly larger than the B bay. It gives no duration ([Code One Magazine](https://www.codeonemagazine.com/article.html?item_id=28)). A Lockheed demo description of the family is the operational sequence in ordinary language: the doors open, the weapon comes out, and the doors close ([Lockheed Martin video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5XXgu5fX864)). The FAA F-35B airshow profile allows the doors to be opened during a specified pass and gives no open or close time ([FAA F-35B airshow profile](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/about/initiatives/airshow/F-35B_Airshow_Flight_Profile.pdf)). The UK form's separate inboard and outboard door-cycle counts show that both exist as life items for Paveway IV. They do not publish how many doors each bay has, the hinge line, or the seconds. The first airborne weapons separation of any F-35, described by Headquarters Marine Corps, was one inert 1,000 lb GBU-32 from BF-3 at **400 knots and 4,200 feet** on 8 August. The extracted text does not include the year, the marines.mil copy returned an access error, and the wording is a search extract of that page ([HQMC release](https://www.hqmc.marines.mil/News/Article/Article/553121/f-35-completes-first-airborne-weapons-separation/)). A NAVAIR account of a March pit-drop series on BF-3, year not stated, covered nine internal combinations of inert GBU-12, GBU-32, and AIM-120 from the left and right bays and ended with a 500 lb ejection. No time between releases, and no statement that two weapons left together, is in that account ([NAVAIR](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/18946)). Those conditions are test points. No public source retrieved prohibits release of these weapons in STOVL, or says the lift-fan doors block a store. Lockheed's airframe descriptions separate the lift-fan doors, dorsal auxiliary inlet, three-bearing swivel-nozzle doors, and wing roll ducts from the weapons bays, and roll posts are not described as deleting a pylon ([Lockheed Martin, F-35 Air Vehicle Technology Overview](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/content/dam/lockheed-martin/eo/documents/webt/F-35_Air_Vehicle_Technology_Overview.pdf)). No flight-manual statement was found that the weapons-bay doors must be closed before conversion, or that release is inhibited with the lift fan engaged. The life-limit form was retrieved only for Paveway IV. Absence of an equivalent form for GBU-12 or GBU-32 is not evidence those stores are unrestricted, and it is not evidence they are banned.

Small weapons and standoff missiles are where a plan count runs ahead of a bay measurement. Boeing's 2015 SDB card says the GBU-39/B fits internal carriage in the F-35 on the BRU-61/A, and gives weapon weight **268 lb**, length **70.8 in**, and width **7.5 in**. The carriage with four weapons is **1,397 lb**, **143 in** long, and **16 in** square. Empty carriage is 325 lb ([Boeing SDB product card, 2015](https://www.boeing.com/content/dam/boeing/v2/products/precision-guided-munitions/documents/small_diameter_bomb_product_card.pdf)). Guidance on the Boeing card is INS/GPS with anti-jam GPS. The Air Force fact sheet says GPS/INS.

GBU-53/B StormBreaker is further along as a test item and still short of a fielding the unclassified record will support. Raytheon's fact sheet gives length **69 in**, wingspan **68 in**, diameter **6–7 in**, and weight **204 lb** ([Raytheon GBU-53/B fact sheet](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/SiteCollectionDocuments/Reports/2010/August%202010/Day25/SDBII_factsheet_0810.pdf)). Early fielding with limited capability on F-35B/C was then intended for FY2024, with IOC in FY2025. Phase I IOC was on the F-15E in September 2022 ([DOT&E FY2023 SDB II](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2023/af/2023sdb.pdf)). Naval News, summarizing the 2025 Marine Corps Aviation Plan on 4 February 2025, said GBU-53 early operational capability was "set to" FY2025 with eight bombs carried internally, and that it would be the first net-enabled weapon fielded on F-35B/C ([Naval News, 4 February 2025](https://www.navalnews.com/naval-news/2025/02/u-s-marine-corps-reshuffles-f-35-plans-in-latest-avplan/)). DOT&E through 30 September 2025 then reported no new F-35 combat capability fielded in that fiscal year. A UK Defence Journal sentence on 3 June 2026, that StormBreaker is already integrated on the F-35B and has been used operationally by Marine Corps aircraft, is not sourced in that article to a Marine Corps or program-office release. It conflicts with DOT&E. The DOT&E cut-off governs through September 2025. The same article says a May 2026 letter approved a UK purchase of GBU-53/B as an interim until SPEAR 3. The letter was not retrieved. Eight internal rounds on the B remain a plan count. The loaded four-round SDB I rack already sits above the 1,000 lb class wording, and no retrieved program document shows the StormBreaker rack inside the B bay.

JSOW is the clearest platform-list disagreement. At about 1,065 lb and 160 inches, JSOW is over the 1,000 lb class wording and longer than a GBU-31. Naming the B as a platform does not say the store fits the B bay. The Block 3F campaign included an AGM-154 release and does not say which variant dropped it.

SPEAR 3 has been carried and has not been released. A DVIDS caption dates the first F-35B flight with four SPEAR 3 missiles to **20 January 2026** at Patuxent River ([DVIDS, 20 January 2026](https://www.dvidshub.net/image/9700531/f-35b-achieves-first-flight-with-uks-precision-long-range-strike-missile)). ## The specification has to leave the blank fields blank

A number that was never printed, and a pair of numbers that were both printed and disagree, are different problems. The pairs to keep side by side are the STOVL rating of stations 4 and 8 at 1,500 lb in 2010 and the 2012 extract that can be read as 2,500 lb, the centerline cell at 1,000 lb and the loaded pod at 1,017 lb, the A magazine at 181 rounds and a later press account of 180, gun rate at 3,000 shots per minute in the installation briefs and up to 3,300 on the 2024 gun sheet, AIM-120D at 356 lb and 358 lb, AIM-120C span at 19 inches and 17 inches, AIM-9X span at 17.6 inches and 13.9 inches, GBU-39 lateral size at 7.5 inches and 6.08 inches, and JSM mass at 917 lb and 887 lb. The higher station rating does not authorize a 2,000 lb weapon in the B bay. The higher gun rate is a gun-sheet capability, and the source that would say which rate the pod uses in service was not found.

The blanks are the fields a specification has to leave as parameters. Door-cycle time, door count per bay, hinge geometry, speed and load-factor limits for the doors, whether both bays open together, and door travel with the gun pod fitted were not published. The published angles are 97 degrees for an AMRAAM launch and about 45 degrees in vertical landing, as a design description, plus the open-release-close sequence. Simultaneous release and ripple interval were not published. SDB and SPEAR are carried in multiples in plans and in one captive flight. That does not publish the spacing between them. An unofficial video estimate of door time is not an official timing. Minimum launch speed, altitude, and Mach for AIM-120, AIM-9X, ASRAAM, Meteor, and AIM-260 on the F-35 were not found. Current Navy and NAVAIR sheets mark AIM-120 and AIM-9X range and speed classified, so the legacy "20+ miles" and the caption "more than 10 miles" are not the specification to implement. Meteor's "120 miles" and "four times the speed of sound" are the Royal Navy's description of the missile in a release that still assigns operational carriage to Typhoon. ASRAAM's "in excess of 25 km" is the older graphic only. Installed B-pod dispersion, and the numeric contract accuracy limit, were not printed. Station coordinates in inches, store-to-store clearances, maximum store diameter, and maximum store length were not printed. The 400 knot and 4,200 foot GBU-32 separation is one early test point.

External-tank gallonage is a blank with several study numbers attached: 426 on a 2010 graphic extract, 460 and 480 in abandoned mid-2000s studies, and 600 in a 2019 study. None is a certified F-35 store, and a 600-gallon figure has also been attributed to Israeli industry work on the F-35I, which is an A-family jet. The flight model already in this project assigns the B's detachable tanks a fuel capacity, a dry mass, a drag term, and structural coordinates. Those implementation figures do not appear in a public fact sheet for a certified tank, and they are not a weapon-station survey. A specification can treat a store as either attached or released. It cannot promote the tank object's capacity, mass, drag area, or coordinates into F-35 data, and it cannot give a weapon a fuel capacity or a feed priority. AIM-260 has no published size, mass, guidance class, or range. Meteor and ASRAAM have no published fin span. AARGM-ER, LRASM, JASSM, GBU-49, GBU-54, and Paveway IV have no published drawing dimensions in the sources retrieved. GPU-9/A and GPU-8/A remain a secondary nomenclature. The Paveway IV life limits exist and sit in a register that was not retrieved. ASRAAM stations 1 and 11 exist in one secondary compilation and in no MoD sentence found here. Four internal Meteors, eight SDB rounds demonstrated inside the B bay, an internal AIM-9X, an internal B gun, a C pod on a B, and a Sidekick load on a B are magazine or plan statements that the B's own primary record does not support.

Two categories are published as exclusions. An F-35 program spokesman told Breaking Defense that certain F-35As were operationally certified on **12 October 2023** to carry the B61-12, and that the certification does not extend to the F-35B or F-35C. He said Block 4 is not required for that F-35A capability ([Breaking Defense, March 2024](https://breakingdefense.com/2024/03/exclusive-f-35a-officially-certified-to-carry-nuclear-bomb/)). The Public Accounts Committee, on 31 October 2025, said only the F-35A can carry nuclear weapons, and that the UK's announced nuclear role is for 12 F-35As, alongside a stated intention to buy 15 more F-35Bs ([Committee of Public Accounts](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5901/cmselect/cmpubacc/1232/report.html)). This record stops at the variant limit and the date. Electronic attack is named by the RAF as something the pilot can employ from the airplane, next to the AN/APG-81, an infrared search-and-track function, a countermeasure suite, the AN/AAQ-37 Distributed Aperture System, an electro-optical targeting system, Link 16, and MADL ([Royal Air Force, Lightning F-35B](https://www.raf.mod.uk/aircraft/current-aircraft/lightning-f35b/)). No government source retrieved names an external electronic-attack pod as a fielded store on a Marine Corps, UK, or Italian F-35B. DOT&E FY2025 places new electromagnetic-warfare capability in the Block 4 test plan for TR-3 aircraft from production lot 19, inside software builds that were not suitable for operational test. No technique, frequency, or radar-cross-section number is published. Open doors are the public moment of release. A measured change in signature when they move is not.

## Conclusion

The F-35B's public record supports three operator defaults, and they are not the same jet. A Marine Corps load that stays inside named expenditures and the published standard internal set is two AIM-120s with two GBU-32s, or two AIM-120s with two GBU-12s, with external AIM-9Xs and the centerline pod as Block 3F additions whose station-level paperwork was never reprinted for 2026. A UK load uses Paveway IV and ASRAAM. ASRAAM is cleared, and the 2022 answer does not give its station. Italy's primary record names missions, and a secondary briefing account is the only place AIM-120C and GBU-12 appear on an Italian F-35B. Stores past that list fail a different test in each case: GBU-31 and JSM fail the bay, StormBreaker's eight-round plan fails a demonstrated fit, Meteor and SPEAR 3 fail the calendar, and AARGM-ER and LRASM have not left the plan. Through 30 September 2025 that calendar was still a Block 4 schedule aimed, in GAO's September 2025 account, at 2031 for a reduced set, with combat-capable TR-3 aircraft described as a 2026 plan, and with no delivery report in the sources. The thirteen months after the DOT&E cut-off are simply unclosed in this record.

What the specification inherits from that split is a discipline about numbers. Bay class, station rating, payload card, and clearance status each remove a different famous load, which is why an F-35A beast-mode total, a six-missile Sidekick bay, or a nuclear store cannot be copied onto the B and still be this airplane. Where two official figures differ by a few pounds or a few hundred rounds per minute, both belong in the data and a third, averaged figure does not. Where the sources never printed a time, an envelope, a tank, or a station coordinate, the honest field is blank.
